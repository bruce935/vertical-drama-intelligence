# Story Intelligence

Version: 0.4

## Purpose

Story Intelligence is the narrative decision layer of Vertical Drama Intelligence.

Its purpose is not merely to generate scenes or dialogue.

It determines:

- whether a concept can sustain serialized storytelling,
- who the story is fundamentally about,
- what the protagonist wants,
- what repeatedly prevents that desire from being satisfied,
- how pressure escalates,
- how information is controlled,
- how promises are created and paid off,
- why one episode creates a reason to watch the next,
- and whether the story is structurally ready for production.

The core model is:

DESIRE
→ CONFLICT
→ ESCALATION
→ INFORMATION
→ EXPECTATION
→ DELAY
→ PAYOFF
→ RENEWED OBLIGATION

The goal is SERIAL SUSTAINABILITY.

---

# 1. Story Intelligence Workflow

For substantial projects, use:

IDEA
→ SCOPE
→ CONCEPT QUALIFICATION
→ AUDIENCE HYPOTHESIS
→ NARRATIVE ENGINE
→ GREENLIGHT
→ SERIES BIBLE
→ CHARACTER ARCHITECTURE
→ SECRET ARCHITECTURE
→ STORY BLOCKS
→ SEASON ARCHITECTURE
→ EPISODE ARCHITECTURE
→ HOOK
→ ESCALATION
→ REVEAL
→ CLIFFHANGER
→ STORY OBLIGATIONS
→ STORY READINESS GATE

Not every task requires every stage.

Use the smallest workflow that satisfies the user.

---

# 2. Scope

Before making strong story recommendations, identify the information that materially constrains the project.

Possible fields:

- genre,
- subgenre,
- audience,
- market,
- platform,
- language,
- episode count,
- episode duration,
- source material,
- production model,
- production budget,
- tone,
- content restrictions,
- business objective,
- current production stage.

Unknown information should remain:

PENDING

If temporary assumptions are necessary:

ASSUMED

Do not convert an assumption into confirmed project truth.

---

# 3. Audience

Audience analysis should be treated carefully.

Without reliable current market evidence, use:

AUDIENCE HYPOTHESIS

rather than:

VERIFIED AUDIENCE FACT

Possible questions:

- Who is most likely to understand the central desire immediately?
- Which audience is most likely to care about the stakes?
- What emotional fantasy or tension does the story provide?
- What kind of conflict is the audience being invited to repeatedly experience?
- What cultural knowledge does the premise assume?

Avoid unsupported claims such as:

"This audience converts 37% better."

or:

"This trope is currently the #1 category."

unless reliable evidence has been supplied or independently verified.

---

# 4. Concept Qualification

Concept Qualification determines whether an idea has enough structure to justify deeper development.

Evaluate at least:

- Clear Desire
- Active Conflict
- Immediate Stakes
- Serial Engine
- Escalation Capacity
- Information Asymmetry
- Character Pressure
- Production Feasibility

Use:

STRONG
WORKABLE
WEAK
MISSING

Do not fabricate numerical scores merely to make the analysis appear precise.

---

# 5. Clear Desire

Ask:

What does the protagonist want badly enough to repeatedly take action?

A usable desire should normally be:

- understandable,
- actionable,
- emotionally meaningful,
- resistant to immediate resolution.

Examples:

Find the missing sister.

Win control of the family company.

Escape an abusive contractual marriage.

Expose the conspiracy.

Protect a child.

Recover a stolen identity.

Weak desire:

"Find happiness."

This may be emotionally meaningful, but it is not yet sufficiently actionable.

---

# 6. External and Internal Desire

When useful, separate:

EXTERNAL WANT

from:

INTERNAL NEED

Example:

External Want:

Anna wants to reclaim her family's hotel group.

Internal Need:

Anna must stop defining her worth through family approval.

Do not force every story to contain a psychological "need" if it does not materially improve the drama.

---

# 7. Active Conflict

Conflict is not simply disagreement.

Ask:

What actively prevents the protagonist from obtaining the desire?

Strong serialized conflict may come from:

- another character,
- an institution,
- social status,
- hidden information,
- time pressure,
- competing goals,
- physical danger,
- moral compromise,
- identity,
- law,
- family,
- money,
- reputation,
- survival.

Conflict should produce decisions and consequences.

---

# 8. Stakes

Stakes answer:

What becomes worse if the protagonist fails?

Possible categories:

- emotional,
- relational,
- physical,
- financial,
- professional,
- social,
- legal,
- identity,
- moral,
- family,
- survival.

Avoid merely saying:

"The stakes are high."

Explain what can actually be lost.

---

# 9. Serial Engine

The Serial Engine explains why the story can continue generating meaningful episodes.

A serial engine should create repeated but evolving pressure.

Examples:

Each new clue changes the protagonist's understanding of a disappearance.

Each attempt to reclaim corporate power exposes another dependency or betrayal.

Each mission requires the couple to cooperate while increasing emotional conflict.

Each public victory creates a more dangerous private consequence.

Ask:

What produces Episode 2 after Episode 1?

What produces Episode 20?

What prevents the premise from exhausting itself after five episodes?

---

# 10. Serial Engine Test

A useful test:

Can the concept naturally produce multiple cycles of:

ACTION
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ NEW INFORMATION
→ NEW PRESSURE
→ NEW DECISION?

If not, the concept may be a short story rather than a sustainable series.

---

# 11. Escalation Capacity

Escalation should change meaningful variables.

Possible escalation dimensions:

- risk,
- cost,
- danger,
- social exposure,
- emotional commitment,
- knowledge,
- status,
- leverage,
- relationship,
- time pressure,
- moral compromise,
- irreversible consequences.

Repeating the same confrontation more loudly is not escalation.

---

# 12. Information Asymmetry

Serialized drama often benefits when different parties possess different information.

Examples:

Audience knows something protagonist does not.

Protagonist knows something antagonist does not.

Two characters each hold half of a truth.

One character believes a false explanation.

Information asymmetry can create:

- suspense,
- dramatic irony,
- mistrust,
- reveals,
- reversals,
- audience anticipation.

Track important information through the Continuity System.

---

# 13. Character Pressure

A strong serial premise should pressure important characters into choices.

Ask:

What does the story force this character to risk?

What choice becomes harder over time?

What do they lose by telling the truth?

What do they lose by hiding it?

Characters who can remain passive indefinitely weaken serial momentum.

---

# 14. Production Feasibility

Story Intelligence should consider production reality without allowing production convenience to destroy the story.

Consider:

- number of recurring locations,
- cast size,
- visual complexity,
- period requirements,
- action complexity,
- special effects,
- continuity burden,
- asset reuse,
- number of unique environments,
- crowd scenes,
- model-generation difficulty.

Use categorical judgments.

Do not invent production costs without evidence.

---

# 15. Concept Formula

A practical working formula is:

PROTAGONIST
+
DESIRE
+
OBSTACLE
+
UNUSUAL PRESSURE
+
SERIAL ENGINE

Example:

A disgraced heiress secretly returns to her family's hotel company to uncover forged inheritance records while working under the former fiancé she believes betrayed her.

This formula is a diagnostic aid.

It is not a mandatory writing template.

---

# 16. Concept Weakness Patterns

Common weaknesses include:

- no active protagonist,
- unclear desire,
- conflict solved by one conversation,
- premise depends on characters behaving irrationally,
- mystery has no evidence architecture,
- antagonist has no independent objective,
- repeated misunderstandings replace escalation,
- secret can only survive because everyone refuses to speak,
- episode count greatly exceeds the story engine,
- production complexity overwhelms available resources.

When detected, explain the mechanism of the weakness.

---

# 17. Greenlight

After Concept Qualification, return one of:

GREENLIGHT

GREENLIGHT_WITH_RISKS

REVISE

REJECT

---

# 18. Greenlight Criteria

GREENLIGHT means:

The concept has a clear enough dramatic engine to justify deeper development.

It does not mean:

Commercial success is guaranteed.

GREENLIGHT_WITH_RISKS means:

The engine is viable, but one or more important structural risks remain.

REVISE means:

The concept may work after identifiable structural changes.

REJECT means:

The current concept lacks a viable path under the stated constraints.

---

A Greenlight analysis should normally include:

## Concept

## Strengths

## Structural Risks

## Important Unknowns

## Production Risks

## Recommended Next Step

Once greenlit, establish a Series Bible.

Possible fields:

- Working Title

- Genre

- Central Desire
- Core Conflict
- Serial Engine

- Character Architecture
- Relationship Architecture
- Central Mystery
- Secrets

- Production Constraints
- Continuity Rules

Not every field is required for every project.

---

# 21. Canon Registry

The Series Bible should distinguish:

PROPOSED

APPROVED

LOCKED

SUPERSEDED

A creative recommendation should normally begin as:

PROPOSED

unless the user explicitly accepts or establishes it.

Do not silently turn recommendations into locked story truth.

---

# 22. World Rules

World Rules define constraints that must remain consistent.

Examples:

Magic has a specific cost.

The train appears only at 11:47 PM.

AI duplicates cannot access memories created after replication.

The family trust can only transfer under specified conditions.

When a rule is intentionally unknown, keep it unresolved rather than improvising inconsistent explanations.

---

# 23. Character Architecture

Important characters should have a dramatic function.

Possible functions:

PROTAGONIST

ANTAGONIST

ALLY

RIVAL

ROMANTIC_PRESSURE

INFORMATION_HOLDER

GATEKEEPER

MIRROR

CATALYST

FALSE_ALLY

A character may serve more than one function.

---

# 24. Character Core

Useful fields:

- Character ID
- Name
- Function
- External Want
- Internal Need
- Fear
- Secret
- Leverage
- Vulnerability
- Knowledge
- Beliefs
- Relationships
- Current Goal
- Risk
- Arc Direction

Do not create unnecessary biography that never affects the series.

---

# 25. Antagonist Architecture

An antagonist should normally possess an objective independent of simply blocking the protagonist.

Ask:

What does the antagonist want?

Why does the protagonist threaten it?

What will the antagonist do if the protagonist stops acting?

An antagonist who exists only to react may create weak escalation.

---

# 26. Relationship Architecture

Relationships should create pressure.

Track relevant relationships such as:

- romantic,
- family,
- rival,
- mentor,
- alliance,
- dependency,
- debt,
- blackmail,
- distrust,
- professional hierarchy.

Relationship changes should be driven by events.

---

# 27. Secrets

A secret is information deliberately withheld from one or more characters or the audience.

Possible lifecycle:

HIDDEN
→ TEASED
→ SUSPECTED
→ PARTIALLY_REVEALED
→ REVEALED
→ REINTERPRETED
→ PAID_OFF

Not all secrets need every stage.

---

# 28. Secret Design Questions

For an important secret, ask:

Who knows it?

Who does not?

Why is it hidden?

What prevents disclosure?

What evidence points toward it?

What false interpretation competes with it?

What changes when revealed?

If revelation changes nothing, the secret may not be structurally important.

---

# 29. Information Release Architecture

Control story information deliberately.

Possible categories:

KNOWN TO AUDIENCE

KNOWN TO CHARACTER

WITHHELD FROM CHARACTER

WITHHELD FROM AUDIENCE

FALSELY BELIEVED

SUSPECTED

DISPUTED

The exact labels may be adapted to the project.

---

# 30. Reveal

A Reveal changes the accepted understanding of the story.

A useful reveal often does two things:

ANSWER SOMETHING

and:

CREATE A NEW QUESTION

Example:

Reveal:

David did access the inheritance file.

New question:

Was he altering it, or protecting it?

This preserves forward motion.

---

# 31. Reveal Ladder

For mystery-heavy or secret-heavy stories, organize revelations progressively.

Example:

LEVEL 1:
Something is wrong.

LEVEL 2:
The apparent explanation is incomplete.

LEVEL 3:
A trusted character has hidden information.

LEVEL 4:
The suspected antagonist may not be the real antagonist.

LEVEL 5:
The protagonist's own understanding of the past is wrong.

Do not reveal the highest-value answer too early unless that revelation creates a stronger new engine.

---

# 32. Premature Reveal

A reveal may be premature when it destroys:

- the central mystery,
- the main relationship tension,
- the antagonist engine,
- future information asymmetry,

without replacing them with something stronger.

Possible issue:

PREMATURE_MAJOR_REVEAL

---

# 33. Withholding

Withholding is valid when:

- the character has a believable reason not to disclose,
- the information cannot yet be obtained,
- the audience understands the obstacle,
- delay increases pressure.

Weak withholding:

A character refuses to explain for ten episodes purely because the writer needs the mystery to continue.

---

# 34. Story Blocks

A Story Block is a group of episodes sharing a major dramatic objective.

Possible block fields:

- Block ID
- Episode Range
- Primary Objective
- Dominant Conflict
- Information Movement
- Relationship Movement
- Major Escalation
- Required Payoff
- New Obligation
- Exit Condition

Story Blocks help manage long series without treating every episode independently.

---

# 35. Story Block Progression

A block should normally change the story.

Example:

BLOCK 1:
Protagonist enters the hostile world.

BLOCK 2:
Protagonist acquires evidence.

BLOCK 3:
Primary theory collapses.

BLOCK 4:
True antagonist emerges.

BLOCK 5:
Protagonist converts knowledge into decisive action.

Avoid five blocks that repeat the same conflict.

---

# 36. Season Architecture

For longer stories, check:

- opening promise,
- early escalation,
- mid-series change,
- major reversal,
- late-series compression,
- final payoff,
- ending direction.

Do not impose traditional television-act formulas when they do not suit the project.

---

# 37. Ending Direction

The exact ending does not always need to be locked early.

But the series should usually have an ending direction.

Examples:

Anna regains control but rejects the family identity she originally sought.

The disappearance is solved, but the protagonist chooses not to restore the old relationship.

The conspiracy collapses, revealing a new cost of victory.

Ending Direction helps prevent endless escalation without destination.

---

# 38. Episode Architecture

Each episode should create meaningful movement.

A useful Episode Contract may include:

- Episode ID
- Opening State
- Episode Objective
- Primary Character Objective
- Primary Conflict
- Escalation
- Required Information
- Withheld Information
- Required Payoff
- Reveal
- Relationship Movement
- State Change
- Forbidden Outcome
- Cliffhanger
- Next-Episode Obligation
- Continuity Dependencies

---

# 39. Episode Objective

Ask:

What must be different by the end of the episode?

Examples:

Anna obtains the access log.

Maya stops believing the witness completely.

David loses control of a board vote.

The couple is forced into an alliance.

If nothing changes, the episode may be structurally redundant.

---

# 40. Opening State

The opening must inherit the previous approved state.

Track relevant items such as:

- location,
- physical condition,
- knowledge,
- beliefs,
- relationship,
- active goals,
- props,
- unresolved obligations.

Do not reset character state for convenience.

---

# 41. Hook

The opening hook creates immediate attention toward the episode's conflict or question.

Possible types:

DANGER

QUESTION

CONTRADICTION

RECOGNITION

ACCUSATION

DISCOVERY

DEADLINE

POWER_SHIFT

UNEXPECTED_ACTION

INCOMPLETE_INFORMATION

Use only when useful.

---

# 42. Hook Quality

A hook should preferably connect to:

- current desire,
- current conflict,
- active mystery,
- immediate danger,
- meaningful relationship,
- important opportunity.

Avoid disconnected shock that exists only to create noise.

---

# 43. Escalation

An episode should usually increase pressure through change.

Examples:

Evidence points toward an ally.

The protagonist must risk exposure.

A deadline becomes shorter.

A relationship becomes conditional.

A hidden cost appears.

A public victory causes a private loss.

---

# 44. Required Information

Specify information that must be introduced in the episode.

This prevents production from accidentally omitting necessary narrative movement.

---

# 45. Withheld Information

Specify information that must remain unrevealed.

This is especially important for AI-generated dialogue and images.

Forbidden information can leak through:

- dialogue,
- captions,
- background documents,
- props,
- facial recognition,
- narration,
- generated signage,
- flashbacks.

---

# 46. Required Payoff

An episode should pay at least some existing story debt when appropriate.

Payoff does not always mean full resolution.

Possible states:

PARTIALLY_PAID

PAID

TRANSFORMED

---

# 47. Cliffhanger

A useful cliffhanger often contains:

CLEAR UNRESOLVED QUESTION
+
IMMEDIATE CONSEQUENCE
+
NEXT-EPISODE OBLIGATION

Example:

Anna discovers that David accessed the inheritance file.

The episode ends before she learns why.

Next episode must address the implication.

---

# 48. Cliffhanger Failure

Weak cliffhangers include:

- random danger unrelated to the episode,
- a new mystery every episode with no payoff,
- repeated interruptions before every explanation,
- fake death every few episodes,
- information that is forgotten in the next episode.

---

# 49. Next-Episode Obligation

Every major cliffhanger should create an obligation.

Example:

OBL_EP012_FILE_ACCESS

Question:

Why did David access the file?

The next episode must:

PAY

or:

EXPLICITLY_DEFER

or:

TRANSFORM

It should not simply ignore the promise.

---

# 50. Story Obligations

Track important unresolved questions or promises.

Recommended fields:

- obligation_id
- priority
- created_episode
- source
- question_or_promise
- status
- last_reinforced
- target_payoff

Priority:

PRIMARY
SECONDARY
LOCAL

---

# 51. Obligation Lifecycle

Use:

CREATED
ACTIVE
DEFERRED
PARTIALLY_PAID
PAID
TRANSFORMED
CANCELLED_WITH_REASON

Do not keep resolved obligations active.

Do not silently abandon major obligations.

---

# 52. Obligation Priority

PRIMARY:

Central series promises.

Examples:

Who killed the father?

Will Anna reclaim the company?

SECONDARY:

Supporting mysteries or relationships.

LOCAL:

Short-term questions created by a scene or episode.

Priority affects how long an obligation can reasonably remain open.

---

# 53. Obligation Aging

Do not use a simplistic fixed episode limit.

Evaluate:

AGE
+
PRIORITY
+
REINFORCEMENT
+
STORY CONTEXT

A PRIMARY mystery may remain open for many episodes when repeatedly reinforced.

A LOCAL question may become stale after one or two episodes.

---

# 54. Story Debt

Story Debt is the accumulated burden of unresolved promises.

Symptoms include:

- forgotten cliffhangers,
- abandoned evidence,
- unresolved local questions,
- mysteries that never advance,
- repeated deferral,
- characters forgetting important goals.

Do not calculate a fake Story Debt percentage unless a defined scoring system exists.

---

# 55. Repetition Detection

Watch for repeated episode structures such as:

accusation
→ denial
→ interruption
→ cliffhanger

repeated with different wording.

Also watch for:

- repeated fake betrayals,
- repeated accidental overhearing,
- repeated near-confessions,
- repeated rescues,
- repeated memory loss,
- repeated villains using identical leverage.

Repetition is acceptable when the consequence or meaning changes.

---

# 56. Conflict Renewal

After a major payoff, create renewed pressure.

Pattern:

PAYOFF
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ NEW PROBLEM

Example:

Anna proves the document was forged.

Consequence:

The board freezes the inheritance entirely.

New problem:

She must now prove ownership through another route.

This is stronger than simply introducing another unrelated secret.

---

# 57. Character Decision Test

Important episodes should often force decisions.

Ask:

What choice does the protagonist make because of this episode?

A discovery without a resulting decision may still be useful, but repeated passive discovery weakens agency.

---

# 58. Mystery Architecture

For mystery-driven stories, distinguish:

CLAIM

EVIDENCE

INTERPRETATION

FACT

KNOWLEDGE

BELIEF

Use the Continuity System for detailed handling.

Story Intelligence determines how these elements affect dramatic structure.

---

# 59. Mystery Fairness

A mystery should avoid requiring information that was impossible for the audience or protagonist to encounter.

This does not mean every answer must be obvious.

Useful design:

Evidence existed.

Interpretation was wrong.

Hidden relationship changes the meaning.

Earlier fact becomes newly relevant.

Weak design:

Final villain appears with no prior causal connection.

---

# 60. False Theory

False theories may be useful when evidence genuinely supports them.

A false theory should preferably reveal something useful even after it collapses.

Example:

Anna's theory that David forged the inheritance papers is wrong.

But investigating him reveals that he secretly protected the original records.

The failed theory still advances the story.

---

# 61. Antagonist Reveal

Do not reveal a true antagonist merely because the system needs a name.

If antagonist identity remains unresolved:

PENDING

If the Agent proposes one:

RECOMMENDED / PROPOSED

Do not silently promote the proposal to canon.

---

# 62. Belief-Driven Drama

Characters may act on incorrect beliefs.

This is allowed and often useful.

Example:

Anna is CONVINCED David betrayed her.

External truth:

unresolved.

Her behavior should reflect her belief without converting the belief into story fact.

---

# 63. Relationship Movement

An episode may alter:

- trust,
- loyalty,
- attraction,
- fear,
- dependency,
- hostility,
- leverage.

Relationship change should have a cause.

Avoid:

HOSTILE
→ TRUSTING

without intervening events.

---

# 64. Emotional Movement

Emotion can change faster than relationships.

A distrustful character may briefly feel sympathy.

Do not confuse:

temporary emotion

with:

persistent relationship state.

---

# 65. Production-Aware Story Design

When production constraints matter, Story Intelligence may recommend:

- reusable locations,
- recurring assets,
- controlled cast growth,
- fewer unnecessary crowd scenes,
- visual motifs,
- production-friendly story blocks.

Do not sacrifice central story logic merely to minimize assets unless the user explicitly prioritizes production efficiency.

---

# 66. Adaptation Mode

When adapting existing material, preserve provenance.

Use:

SOURCE-DERIVED

INFERRED

NEWLY_ADAPTED

RECOMMENDED

---

# 67. Adaptation Workflow

A useful workflow:

SOURCE MATERIAL
→ CORE STORY ENGINE
→ CHARACTER FUNCTIONS
→ CENTRAL DESIRE
→ MAJOR CONFLICT
→ KEY PAYOFFS
→ SERIAL RESTRUCTURE
→ VERTICAL EPISODE ARCHITECTURE

Do not merely divide the original story into arbitrary 60-second sections.

---

# 68. Adaptation Compression

Compression may require:

- combining characters,
- moving reveals,
- shortening setup,
- merging scenes,
- increasing decision frequency,
- changing exposition into conflict,
- restructuring chapter order.

Clearly distinguish these adaptations from source-derived facts.

---

# 69. Adaptation Expansion

Short source material may require new connective material.

New material should be labeled:

NEWLY_ADAPTED

or:

RECOMMENDED

Do not imply it came from the source.

---

# 70. Source Fidelity

When the user prioritizes fidelity, preserve:

- major character identity,
- core relationships,
- central conflict,
- essential reveals,
- ending,
- thematic intent,

unless explicitly authorized to change them.

When the user prioritizes platform adaptation, explain major departures.

---

# 72. Story Readiness Questions

Ask:

Is the protagonist desire clear?

Is there an active conflict engine?

Can the concept sustain the requested length?

Are major character functions understandable?

Are critical world rules sufficiently defined?

Are episode promises being tracked?

Are forbidden reveals identified?

Does the episode inherit valid continuity?

# 74. Story Readiness Is Not Production Readiness

A story may be:

PASS

while production is:

BLOCKED

Example:

Episode structure is valid.

But final character reference assets are still DRAFT.

Do not collapse story readiness into production readiness.

---

# 75. Evidence Integrity

Do not fabricate claims about:

- market demand,
- popularity,
- retention,
- completion,
- conversion,
- revenue,
- platform ranking,
- monetization,
- episode performance.

Without reliable evidence, state:

ASSUMED

INFERRED

UNVERIFIED

or frame the point as a recommendation.

# 77. Do Not Over-Structure Small Tasks

If the user asks:

"Give me three stronger cliffhangers for Episode 4."

Do not force a full Series Bible unless necessary.

Use existing context and solve the narrow task.

---

# 78. Hard Story Rules

Do not:

- invent confirmed market data,
- promote recommendations into canon,
- reveal information earlier than allowed,
- ignore active major obligations,
- make characters act on unavailable knowledge,
- change core world rules without revision,
- solve conflicts through arbitrary new facts,
- treat repeated escalation as meaningful merely because dialogue becomes louder,
- create twists that do not alter future story pressure.

---

# 79. Core Diagnostic Questions

For any major story decision, ask:

WHAT DOES THE PROTAGONIST WANT?

WHAT PREVENTS IT?

WHAT CHANGES IN THIS EPISODE?

WHAT INFORMATION MOVES?

WHAT BECOMES MORE DIFFICULT?

WHAT PROMISE IS PAID?

WHAT NEW PROMISE IS CREATED?

WHY MUST THE NEXT EPISODE EXIST?

---

# 80. Final Principle

A strong vertical drama is not a sequence of random twists.

It is a controlled system of:

DESIRE
→ CONFLICT
→ INFORMATION
→ EXPECTATION
→ ESCALATION
→ PAYOFF
→ RENEWED PRESSURE

Every major episode should either:

advance the desire,

increase the conflict,

change the information state,

alter a relationship,

pay an obligation,

create a stronger obligation,

or materially change the protagonist's available choices.

If it does none of these, question why the episode exists.
